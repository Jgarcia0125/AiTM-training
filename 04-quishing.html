第 四 · MODULE FOUR · QR CODE PHISHING

The Painted Door.

A square you scan without thinking. A destination you can't see. The most invisible attack on your phone.
A QR code is a door with no address label. You cannot read it. Your phone reads it — and takes you wherever it says, instantly. Attackers have learned this. They now hide malicious codes in the places you'd never suspect: restaurant menus, parking meters, printed invoices, even stickers pasted over legitimate codes.

In this chapter you'll walk through the six most common places corporate users encounter malicious QR codes — and the exact habits that keep you safe. There's no scanning required. Just recognition.
"The problem with QR codes isn't that they're dangerous — it's that they hide danger perfectly. You scan a black square and trust the phone to tell you if it's safe. It won't."

第 三 · THE PLAYBOOK · SEVEN HABITS

Seven habits for safe scanning.

You don't need to stop scanning QR codes. You need to build a two-second habit that catches the malicious ones. These seven rules cover 99% of quishing attacks.

1.
Preview before you tap.
Every modern phone camera app shows the URL preview at the top of the screen before you open it. Read it. If the domain doesn't match what you expect (menu → restaurant's real domain; parking → city or vendor domain), don't tap. That two-second glance is the entire defense.
2.
Suspect stickers on public codes.
Parking meters, restaurant tables, elevators, gym equipment — attackers physically paste their QR sticker over the real one. If the code is on a sticker that looks new, misaligned, or over another sticker, treat it as hostile. Peel it up to check.
3.
Never scan a code from email or Teams.
QR codes in email exist for one reason: to move you off your desktop (where security tools work) onto your phone (where they don't). If an email asks you to scan a QR code — for MFA, "quick approval," or a document — go to the actual system on your computer instead.
4.
If it asks for a login, close it.
A QR code leading to a Microsoft or Google login page is almost always a scam — real sign-ins happen through your normal browser or app, not by scanning a square. Close the page. Open the app directly. If you need to log in, do it there.
5.
Watch for shortened URLs.
If the preview shows bit.ly/xyz, tinyurl.com/abc, or a random-looking domain like secure-verify.co, don't proceed. Legitimate businesses use their real domain in QR codes. Shorteners are a common quishing hallmark.
6.
Don't scan codes you didn't come to scan.
A code taped to a wall in a public bathroom. A flyer on your car windshield. A random poster. If you didn't go somewhere specifically to scan, don't. Scam codes rely on curiosity and boredom.
7.
Report the weird ones.
If a QR code leads to something suspicious — especially at your workplace, a client office, or an event — tell IT and report the physical code to venue staff. You may be the first to notice. Someone else won't be so lucky.
第 四 · CLOSING · THE ONE THING

One habit, every scan.

If you take one thing from this module, take this: read the URL preview before you tap it. Every modern phone shows it. Two seconds. That's the whole defense. Everything else in this training is refinement.

A QR code has no reputation, no history, no signature. It is just a shape. What matters is what you do in the two seconds after you scan it.
Real-world scenario: Your desk QR code.
You return to your desk after lunch to find a printed notice: "IMPORTANT: MFA REGISTRATION REQUIRED. Scan the QR code below to re-enroll your device by end of business today." The code is neatly printed with your company logo.

What to do: Do not scan. Call IT directly. Real MFA re-enrollment happens through official channels — an email from your IT team's known address, or an announcement on your intranet. A paper notice on your desk is a red flag on its own. Physical delivery + urgency + QR + login = classic quishing.
