The Human Ledger

A weekly dispatch on the small deceptions costing companies millions
Cover Story · Est. Reading Time 8 min

"Are you at your desk? I need a quick favor."

The message arrives at 4:47 PM on a Friday. Your CEO's name is on it. Something feels urgent, personal, quiet. In the next ten minutes, an employee somewhere in the country will lose their company between four and seventy thousand dollars — because they wanted to be helpful.

Business Email Compromise is not a technical attack. It's a social one — designed to make a good employee do a bad thing before their better judgment catches up.

In this training you'll see a real-world BEC unfold as a text and email exchange. At each turn you'll decide what to do. There are no trick questions — only patterns to recognize, and habits to build.

Scene 01 / The Opening

A text from an unknown number.

You're wrapping up for the weekend. Your phone lights up. It's not a saved contact — but the person seems to know you, and the name they mention is your CEO's. Read carefully. What do you do?

?
+1 (346) 555-0129
Unknown Number
Friday 4:47 PM
Hi, are you available? I need your help with something quick. — David
This is David Chen, your CEO. I got a new phone and lost some contacts.
◀
Signals to Read
SIGNAL 01
Unknown number, familiar name
A real executive doesn't text you from a number you don't have saved. The "new phone" explanation is the most common opener in BEC.
SIGNAL 02
Off-channel contact
Real business happens in email, Teams, Slack — channels that leave a record. Personal text = red flag.
SIGNAL 03
Vague urgency
"Quick help," "are you available," "need a favor" — the request is deliberately foggy. That fog is a tool.
SIGNAL 04
Late Friday afternoon
Attackers time these for when you're mentally checked out and IT staff have gone home. Weekends and holidays are peak BEC hours.

What's your move?

A common instinct — but it's what the attacker wants.
Replying confirms your number is active and that you believe the story. From here, the attacker gets to shape the conversation. The right move is to verify through a known channel — Teams, Slack, or David's known desk line — before you engage. Reveal all four signals above to see everything wrong with the opening message.
That's the right instinct.
Verifying through a known channel (Teams, Slack, David's known desk phone) is the single most effective defense against BEC. In this scenario the real David is at his desk and never sent a text. But let's follow what would have happened if you'd replied — because most of your coworkers will.
Understandable — but you'd miss the lesson.
Blocking is fine for obvious spam, but the report step matters. Forwarding the message to your IT/Security team helps them warn everyone else — often, when one employee gets a BEC text, dozens do. Report first, block second.
Scene 02 / The Ask

The real request.

Let's say a colleague did reply — this is what would arrive next. Notice how the request becomes concrete, specific, and time-boxed.

?
+1 (346) 555-0129
Unknown Number
Great, thanks for responding! I'm in back-to-back meetings today so I can't take a call.
I need you to pick up some Apple gift cards for a client appreciation gift. It's time-sensitive — client is flying out tonight.
Can you grab 10 x $500 cards from Target on your way home? I'll reimburse Monday. Just send me photos of the codes when done.
Please keep this between us for now — it's a surprise announcement.
◀
The BEC Fingerprint
TELL 01
Gift cards, wire, or crypto
If a request involves gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or changed banking details — treat it as hostile until proven otherwise.
TELL 02
"I can't take a call"
The attacker cannot pass a voice check. This excuse is designed to block the one verification that would expose them.
TELL 03
"Keep this between us"
Isolation is a weapon. Real executives don't ask you to hide business transactions from colleagues.
TELL 04
"I'll reimburse you"
You are being asked to front the money personally. That is never a legitimate business process. Never.
In 2023, gift-card BEC scams cost U.S. businesses over $50 million. Not because CFOs authorized it — because well-meaning employees did.
Scene 03 / The Higher-Stakes Version

The vendor who moved.

Gift-card BEC is the low-stakes version. This one — invoice redirection — is where companies lose six or seven figures at a time. It arrives quietly. It looks routine. Read every line.

From: Jennifer Marks <jmarks@acme-corp.net>
To: ap@yourcompany.com
Subject: Updated Banking Details for Invoice #INV-4471
Date: Friday, 4:52 PM

Hi team,

I hope you're doing well. I'm reaching out regarding invoice INV-4471 (originally sent 3 weeks ago).

Our accounting firm has changed our banking partner. Please update our details in your system before processing payment:

NEW Bank: First Meridian Trust
Routing: 021000021
Account: 8847290011
Beneficiary: Acme Corp LLC

Please confirm receipt and process at your earliest convenience — we're working to close our books for the month.

Best regards,
Jennifer Marks
Accounts Receivable, Acme Corp

DOMAIN LOOK
acme-corp.net vs .com
The real Acme uses acme-corp.com. This attacker registered acme-corp.net weeks ago.
THE ASK
Banking change on existing invoice
Vendors change banks — but any change should trigger phone verification to a known contact.
TIMING
Late Friday, "closing our books"
Urgency + weekend + specific-sounding reason = classic pressure play.
One phone call — to a number the vendor gave you six months ago, not one in the email — would have caught this. That call is the single most valuable habit in accounts payable.
Debrief / Your Playbook

The five habits that stop BEC.

No technology solves BEC completely. What stops it is a small set of habits that any employee can build. Read these once, and read them again.

1.
Verify the person, not the message.
Any request involving money, credentials, banking changes, or gift cards — call the person on a number you already have, or message them on Teams/Slack. Not the number in the email. Not the number they gave you. The one you already trust.
2.
Assume unusual channels are hostile.
Your CEO doesn't need to text you from an unknown number. Your CFO doesn't ask for wire changes over WhatsApp. A vendor doesn't switch banks via a stranger's Gmail. The channel is often the tell.
3.
Read the domain like you're being paid to.
acme-corp.net vs acme-corp.com. yourcompany.co vs yourcompany.com. Look for extra letters, missing letters, and swapped TLDs. Attackers register lookalike domains hours before they use them.
4.
Urgency is a weapon. Recognize it.
"Before the client leaves," "closing the books," "I'm in a meeting" — these phrases are engineered to bypass your judgment. Whenever you feel rushed by a message, that's the moment to slow down.
5.
Report every suspicious message. Even if unsure.
When one employee gets a BEC message, dozens usually do. Your report protects everyone else. IT would rather see ten false positives than miss the one that empties the treasury.
