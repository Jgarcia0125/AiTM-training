The Voice
on the Line.

A short study in the oldest attack still working — modernized with a phone that knows your name.
Runtime · 8 Minutes · Interactive
Someone calls. They know your name. They know your company. They know your extension. They speak with confidence and courtesy. They ask a simple question, then a slightly harder one, then a request that requires trust. By the time you hang up, the damage is done — and the strangest part is you'd tell your family the call felt normal.
This is vishing: phishing over the phone. It works because voice is the channel your brain trusts most. This module teaches you to distrust it — precisely, and only where it matters.

ACT II · A CALL AT 2:47 PM

The script they use on you.

Below is a real vishing call, reconstructed from actual incidents. Read each line and hover over the TELL markers to see what an attacker is doing at that moment.

▶ CALL RECORDING · TUESDAY 14:47 · INBOUND · 6 MIN 22 SEC MOD.V.SC.02
Caller
Hi, is this Alex? Alex Chen from Accounts Receivable? TELL 01
You
Yes, this is Alex.
Caller
Great — this is Marcus with the Microsoft security team. TELL 02 We're seeing some unusual sign-in activity on your account and I need to walk through some quick verification with you.
You
Oh — is something wrong?
Caller
Nothing to panic about, but we saw a sign-in attempt from Bulgaria this morning TELL 03 and we want to make sure it wasn't you before we lock the account. Can you tell me — are you at your desk right now?
You
Yes, I'm here.
Caller
Perfect. I'm going to send a verification code to your phone right now. TELL 04 When you receive it, please just read me the six-digit number so we can confirm the account is still yours and reject the Bulgarian sign-in.
You
Okay… I just got a text. It says 847293.
Caller
Thank you Alex, we've secured your account. Have a great afternoon. TELL 05
The voice is calm. The story makes sense. The request seems small. That is the entire attack.
ACT III · THE FIVE TELLS

Five signals in every vishing call.

Every vishing call — whether from a "Microsoft engineer," "your bank's fraud team," a "delivery driver," or a "help desk agent" — shares the same skeleton. Learn the skeleton and every disguise looks the same.

I.
They called you.
Legitimate IT, banks, and vendors sometimes do call — but they almost never ask you to do anything sensitive on that call. If someone unexpectedly calls and needs an action from you (a code, a password, a payment, a click), the call itself is the red flag. Hang up, call the organization's real number, and start over.
II.
They already know you.
Name, title, department, sometimes even a recent project. This information is trivially available — LinkedIn, company site, data breaches. Familiarity is not authentication. A stranger knowing your role is not proof they're legitimate.
III.
They introduce urgency, then friendliness.
"Your account will be locked" / "Suspicious activity detected" / "We just need to confirm quickly." The urgency triggers fear. The friendliness makes you cooperative. Together they short-circuit your judgment. Recognize the pattern; it's the attack.
IV.
They ask for a code, a click, or a payment.
No legitimate support call requires you to read out an MFA code, click a link they text you, approve an MFA push, share your password, or make a payment. If they ask, they are attacking you. Every single time. There are no exceptions to this rule.
V.
The voice may not be a person at all.
AI voice cloning now needs three seconds of audio to convincingly impersonate your CEO, your spouse, your child. If a familiar voice makes an unusual request, especially involving money or credentials — verify through a different channel before acting. Ask something only the real person would know.
ACT IV · THE CLOSING

Your standing rules.

Voice attacks work because they're personal, urgent, and hard to verify in the moment. These rules make verification easy and give you an off-ramp from any call that feels off.

RULE ONE
Hang up. Call back.
The single most powerful defense against vishing: end the call, look up the real number of the organization they claimed to be from, and call that number. A real support agent will thank you for verifying. A scammer's number won't route to the real company. This one habit stops 95% of vishing.
RULE TWO
Never share a code.
MFA codes, verification codes, one-time passwords — you never say these out loud to anyone. Not to "Microsoft." Not to "your bank." Not to "IT." A code is a temporary key to your account. Sharing it is handing over the key. There are no legitimate reasons to read a code to a caller.
RULE THREE
Verify a familiar voice.
If someone you know — spouse, child, boss — calls with an unusual money or credential request, verify on another channel before acting. Send them a text at their known number, or ask a question only they would know ("what did we have for dinner Sunday?"). AI cloning is real, cheap, and used daily. Trust the identity, not the sound.
RULE FOUR
Real IT will not mind.
You may feel rude asking a caller to prove who they are. Get over it. Real IT people, real bank fraud teams, real vendor support — they will happily wait while you hang up and call back. If a caller pressures you to stay on the line, or gets angry when you ask to verify, that is proof they are not legitimate.
RULE FIVE
Report the strange calls.
If you get a suspicious call — even if you didn't fall for it — tell IT/Security. Vishing campaigns often hit multiple employees in the same day. Your report is the early warning that protects the coworker who might have said yes. You don't need proof, just the story. Two minutes of your time; hours of theirs saved.
Voice is the last channel that feels real. That's exactly why attackers use it. And exactly why you should trust it least.
