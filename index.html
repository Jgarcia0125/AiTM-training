Stage 01 / 06 — The Bait

An urgent email lands in your inbox.

It's 9:47 AM Tuesday. You're on your second coffee. An email from "Microsoft 365" tells you a shared document needs your review — your manager's name is on it. The subject line says action required. Take a breath. Look closely. There are at least four things wrong with this email. Hover, inspect, and click anything that looks suspicious to flag it.

Detected Red Flags 0/4
FLAG 01 — SENDER
Lookalike domain: rnicrosoft
"rn" mimics "m". Real Microsoft uses microsoft.com — not rnicrosoft-secure.com.
FLAG 02 — URGENCY
Manufactured time pressure
"Expires in 24 hours" or "permanent data loss" is designed to short-circuit your judgment.
FLAG 03 — LINK
Hover reveals the truth
The button says "View Document" but the URL points to a lookalike domain. Always hover before clicking.
FLAG 04 — TRUST CLAIM
Self-declared trust
Microsoft never adds "this is a trusted sender" banners inside the email body. Real trust signals come from your mail client, not the message itself.
Stage 02 / 06 — The Mirror

You land on a perfect copy of the Microsoft sign-in page.

Pixel-perfect. Same fonts. Same layout. The favicon even matches. But this isn't Microsoft. It's a reverse proxy — software like Evilginx that sits between you and the real login server, forwarding every request and stealing every response. Look at the URL. Always look at the URL.

What To Look For
CHECK 01 — URL
login.microsoftonline.com
Microsoft sign-in lives at login.microsoftonline.com or login.live.com. NOT subdomains of weird names.
CHECK 02 — PADLOCK
HTTPS isn't proof of safety
90%+ of phishing sites have valid SSL certs now. The padlock means encrypted — not legitimate.
CHECK 03 — PASSKEYS
If you have a passkey, use it
Passkeys are domain-bound. They simply won't work on the wrong site — your browser refuses to send them.
CHECK 04 — BOOKMARKS
Type or use a saved bookmark
Never reach a login page by clicking an email link. Open a new tab and type the URL or use your bookmark.
Stage 03 / 06 — The Relay

Behind the curtain: your credentials are forwarded live.

You hit submit. To you, the page just loaded. In reality: the attacker's proxy received your username and password, opened a connection to the real Microsoft login server, and pasted them in — all in under 200 milliseconds. The real Microsoft now sends a legitimate MFA prompt to your phone. You're about to approve the attacker's session.

Stage 04 / 06 — The Handoff

You approve. The attacker becomes you.

This is the moment. Your phone buzzes with a real Microsoft Authenticator prompt — because Microsoft really did receive a login from a real-looking source. You approve. Microsoft issues a session cookie. The proxy intercepts it. That cookie is now in the attacker's hands — and a session cookie is a fully authenticated session, no password or MFA required to use it.

Look for these subtle warnings on the MFA screen.

Read The Prompt
SIGNAL 01 — LOCATION
Unfamiliar geography
You're in San Antonio. The login is from Atlanta. If the location doesn't match where you are right now, deny it.
SIGNAL 02 — TIMING
Did YOU just trigger this?
If a prompt arrives when you didn't initiate a login, it's an attack. Always.
SIGNAL 03 — IP / ASN
VPS hosting providers
Attackers run proxies from cloud VPS providers (DigitalOcean, Hetzner, etc.). Microsoft increasingly flags these — but only after damage is done.
SIGNAL 04 — NUMBER MATCH
Number-matching MFA helps
Old-style "Approve / Deny" prompts get tapped reflexively. Number matching forces you to read the screen.
Stage 05 / 06 — The Aftermath

You're in. So is the attacker.

Microsoft redirects you to your real inbox. Everything looks fine. Meanwhile, the attacker imports your stolen session cookie into their browser and is also looking at your real inbox — from Atlanta. They have ~14 days before the cookie expires. Within minutes they'll set up inbox rules to hide their tracks, search for "wire transfer," "invoice," "wallet," and pivot to lateral targets.

⚠ POST-INCIDENT NOTE
Average attacker dwell time after a successful AiTM is 9 minutes for the first malicious action, and under an hour for the first business email compromise attempt. Speed of reporting is the single biggest factor in damage control.
Stage 06 / 06 — Debrief

Your defense playbook.

AiTM works because the attack happens between you and a legitimate service. The attacker doesn't need to be smarter than your security team — they just need you to react faster than you think. Slow down. Memorize this checklist. It is shorter than your password.

01
Hover before you click. Always.
On desktop, hover the link to see where it really goes. On mobile, long-press. If the domain isn't what the email claims, stop. Bonus: typing a known URL or using your bookmark is even better.
02
Read the URL bar like a paranoid lawyer.
Microsoft = login.microsoftonline.com. Look for rn instead of m, 0 instead of o, 1 instead of l, and weird subdomains like microsoft.attacker.com.
03
Use phishing-resistant MFA where you can.
Passkeys, FIDO2 security keys, and Windows Hello are domain-bound — they refuse to authenticate to the wrong site. SMS and OTP codes can all be relayed by an AiTM proxy. Push approvals are the easiest to fool.
04
If a prompt arrives unexpectedly, deny it.
If you didn't just hit "sign in," and your phone is buzzing with an MFA prompt — someone else is trying to sign in as you. Deny, then change your password from a known-good device.
05
Treat urgency as a flag, not a fact.
"Expires in 24 hours," "your account will be suspended," "your CEO needs this NOW" — these are weaponized phrases. Real systems give you reasonable time. Slow down on purpose.
06
Report it — even if you only suspect.
Use the "Report Phishing" button in your mail client. If you think you may have entered credentials, tell IT immediately, even if you're not 100% sure. The first 9 minutes are everything. Embarrassment costs nothing. Silence costs the company.
07
Watch for "consent phishing" too.
After AiTM, attackers often grant a malicious OAuth app permission to read your mail — that gives them access even after you change your password. Audit "My account → Apps" periodically and revoke anything you don't recognize.
Quick check: A pixel-perfect Microsoft login page asks for your password, but the URL is login.microsoftonl1ne.com. You also have a passkey enrolled. What happens?
Correct. Passkeys (and FIDO2 keys) are bound to the exact origin they were registered against. The browser silently refuses to release the credential to login.microsoftonl1ne.com — even though it looks identical. This is why passkeys are called phishing-resistant: humans can be fooled by lookalike URLs, but the cryptographic protocol cannot.
You're now harder to fool.
